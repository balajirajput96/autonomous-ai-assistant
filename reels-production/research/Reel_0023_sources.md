# Reel 0023 — Reward Prediction Error: Source Record

**Working topic:** Why can an unexpected outcome update a learned expectation?  
**Claim category:** Computational reinforcement-learning model with neurophysiological and human-neuroimaging evidence.  
**Research status:** Script-ready source verification complete; this record distinguishes model language from subjective emotion claims.

## Source-verified core boundary

Schultz’s 2016 review defines a reward prediction error as the difference between a received reward and its prediction. It summarizes a canonical experimental response pattern in controlled reward-learning work: many recorded midbrain dopamine neurons increase activity for a better-than-predicted reward, remain near baseline for a fully predicted reward, and show reduced activity for a worse-than-predicted reward. The review links this pattern to Rescorla–Wagner and temporal-difference learning models, while framing the teaching-signal account as a model of reward learning rather than a complete explanation of every feeling or choice.[1]

Colas and colleagues used a high-resolution fMRI reward-learning task in humans and reported results consistent with both state-value and action-value prediction-error signals. Their abstract emphasizes that the precise reinforcement-learning algorithm implemented in the human brain is not yet well determined; the article also notes an imaging-resolution limit on precisely delineating some dopaminergic midbrain subdivisions. The reel therefore uses “consistent with a model” language rather than treating an fMRI correlate as direct proof of a single neural algorithm.[3]

Doll and Daw’s accessible eLife overview explains the standard model as a mismatch between predicted and received reward that can update predictions in model-free learning. It also describes evidence that some reward predictions can incorporate information beyond direct pairings, underscoring why “dopamine equals simple reward” is not a sufficient explanation.[4]

| Safe for the reel | Do not claim |
|---|---|
| An outcome can be better or worse than a learned expectation in a learning model. | “Dopamine is pleasure.” |
| A positive/negative prediction-error term can help update expectations in reinforcement-learning accounts. | Every surprise, disappointment, or craving is a reward prediction error. |
| Carefully controlled recordings and human neuroimaging tasks provide evidence relevant to RPE models. | A single dopamine measurement directly reads a person’s happiness, motivation, or free will. |
| Computational terms can be useful explanatory models. | The model proves a person's subjective experience or diagnoses an individual. |
