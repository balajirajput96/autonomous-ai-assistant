# Reel 0019 — Feelings ko naam dena: kya research kehti hai?

**Topic:** Affect labelling, or putting an affective experience into words.  
**Claim class:** Controlled-task emotion-regulation evidence plus neuroimaging association.  
**Audience question:** “क्या ‘मैं anxious हूँ’ कहने से feeling सच में बदल सकती है?”

## Allowed, evidence-bounded claims

| Reel claim | Evidence boundary | Source support |
|---|---|---|
| Affect labelling means choosing words for an emotion or affective response, such as naming an experience as anxious, sad or angry. | This is a task/process definition, not a diagnosis. | Burklund et al. describe participants selecting labels for their own affective responses while viewing negative images. [1] |
| In laboratory tasks with aversive images, affect labelling has been associated with lower reported distress than passive viewing. | This describes controlled comparisons; it does not promise the same effect in every real-life situation. | Levy-Gigi and Shamay-Tsoory’s 2022 experiments report lower distress in their affect-labelling condition relative to baseline. [2] |
| Context matters: in the 2022 study, the effect differed by image intensity; the authors reported reduced distress in high-intensity conditions and increased distress in low-intensity conditions. | This is one study’s result, not a universal rule for when to label feelings. | Levy-Gigi and Shamay-Tsoory, Experiment 2. [2] |
| Some fMRI studies found affect labelling was associated with reduced amygdala response and altered prefrontal activity during their tasks. | “Associated with” is essential. An fMRI activation pattern does not establish a single causal pathway, a cure, or a universal brain switch. | Lieberman et al. (2007); Burklund et al. (2014). [1] [3] |

## Script boundary

The reel may offer a low-stakes reflection prompt such as **“अभी मैं क्या महसूस कर रहा/रही हूँ—एक शब्द चुनो”**. It must frame this as noticing and describing an experience, not as medical treatment, a replacement for support, or a guaranteed way to remove anxiety.

> **No-script rule:** Do not say that naming an emotion “switches off the amygdala,” “always calms you,” “heals trauma,” or proves the cause of a feeling. The evidence here comes from specific experimental tasks and samples.

## Source notes

1. **Burklund, Creswell, Irwin & Lieberman (2014), _Frontiers in Psychology_.** This fMRI study of healthy older adults compared personally relevant affect labelling, reappraisal and passive observation of negative images. Its abstract reports similar decreases in amygdala activity and correlated reductions in self-reported distress for labelling and reappraisal; it does not establish a treatment effect. [1]
2. **Levy-Gigi & Shamay-Tsoory (2022), _PLOS ONE_.** Two controlled experiments tested timing and intensity in affect-labelling paradigms. The abstract and results give the key moderation boundary: beneficial effects in high-intensity aversive conditions but increased distress in low-intensity conditions in that sample. [2]
3. **Lieberman et al. (2007), _Psychological Science_.** The paper’s abstract reports reduced amygdala and other limbic responses to negative images during affect labelling relative to comparison encoding tasks, alongside increased right ventrolateral prefrontal activity. It explicitly presents a possible pathway, not proof of a singular mechanism. [3]
